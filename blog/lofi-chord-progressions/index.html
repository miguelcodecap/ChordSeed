6 lo-fi chord progressions (and why they sound so chill)

Lo-fi music sounds relaxed and a little nostalgic, like studying late at night with rain on the window. Most of that feeling comes from the chords. Here are six progressions to start with and the reasons they work.

6 min read · The Chordseed Journal · Leer en español

What makes chords sound “lo-fi”?

  • Sevenths and ninths. Instead of plain C or Am, lo-fi uses Cmaj7, Am9, Dm7. The extra notes make chords soft and unresolved.
  • Short loops. Two or four chords repeating over and over, so the listener can relax into them.
  • Jazz movement. Many loops borrow the ii–V–I from jazz, but slowed down.
  • Slow tempo with swing. Usually 70–90 BPM, with drums that feel a little lazy and behind the beat.

1. The descending loop

Each chord steps down by one note. It feels like a slow sigh.

Fmaj7Em7Dm7Cmaj7IV – iii – ii – I in C

2. The jazz cycle

The classic 1–6–2–5. It never feels finished, which is perfect for a loop.

Cmaj7Am7Dm7G7I – vi – ii – V

3. The ii–V–I with ninths

The same jazz move with richer colors. Swap G9 for G13 if you want it even dreamier.

Dm9G9Cmaj9Cmaj9ii – V – I

4. The two-chord minor vamp

Only two chords, but with ninths they sound deep. Great for melodies and samples on top.

Am9Dm9i – iv in A minor

5. The bittersweet minor

The E7 adds a moment of tension before falling back to Am. Moody and cinematic.

Fmaj7E7Am7Am7VI – V – i in A minor

6. The neo-soul lift

The A7 isn’t in the key of C; it’s a secondary dominant that pulls into Dm7 and gives the loop a soulful lift.

Cmaj7A7Dm7G7I – VI7 – ii – V

Tips to make it sound right

  • Play soft. Electric piano or a clean, mellow guitar works best. Avoid strong strumming.
  • Keep voicings close. Move as few fingers as possible between chords so the loop flows.
  • Let it breathe. One chord per bar, sometimes two bars, is plenty.
  • Add texture. Vinyl crackle, rain or tape hiss are part of the genre, but the chords come first.
