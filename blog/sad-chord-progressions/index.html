Sad chord progressions that always work

Some chords sound like rain on a window. Here are seven progressions that reliably feel sad, nostalgic or bittersweet, why they work, and how to make them your own.

6 min read · The Chordseed Journal

Why some chords sound sad

Three things do most of the work:

  • Minor chords. A minor chord has its middle note one fret lower than a major chord. That single fret is the difference between "bright" and "heavy".
  • A bass that walks down. When the lowest note keeps falling step by step, the ear hears it as sinking or letting go.
  • Not going home. Sad songs often avoid the strong "resolution" chord, or reach it late, so the feeling stays open.

The examples below use easy keys (A minor and C major) so you can play them on guitar or piano right away. The Roman numerals tell you the position of each chord in the key, so you can move them to any other key.

1. The minor anthem: i – VI – III – VII

AmFCGin A minor

Probably the most used sad progression in modern pop and rock. It starts on the minor chord, so the song "lives" in a darker place, then climbs through three major chords that sound hopeful, before falling back. That push and pull between sadness and hope is why it works so well for choruses.

2. The same chords, a different heart: vi – IV – I – V

AmFCGin C major

Yes, it's the same four chords as above. What changes is which one feels like home. Start and end your phrase on Am and it sounds sad; land on C and the same chords feel warm. Where you put the weight (longer chords, the first beat of the bar, the end of the chorus) decides the mood.

3. Slow and heavy: i – iv – v

AmDmEmin A minor

Three minor chords in a row. There's no bright chord to escape to, which makes it feel serious and tired, like a ballad at 2 a.m. Swap the last chord for E (major) and you get a stronger pull back to Am, more dramatic and classical.

4. The Andalusian descent: i – VII – VI – V

AmGFEin A minor

Four chords walking straight down. It sounds like flamenco, film noir and dramatic rock all at once. The final E major creates tension that begs to go back to Am, so it loops forever without getting boring.

5. The bittersweet minor iv: I – IV – iv – I

CFFmCin C major

This is the "happy song that suddenly hurts" trick. You play the normal F, then the same chord in minor (Fm). Only one note changes, A drops to A♭, but it feels like a cloud passing over the sun. It's borrowed from the minor key, which is why it sounds surprising.

6. The falling bass line

CG/BAmAm/GFin C major

Look at the lowest note of each chord: C, B, A, G, F. It walks down step by step while the chords above stay simple. The slash (G/B) just means "play G, but with B in the bass". On guitar, you can do it by moving one finger on the low strings. It sounds like memories, which is why so many ballads use it.

7. Ending without going home

AmFCGFend on F

Finish a phrase on a chord that isn't the home chord. The song doesn't "close", and the listener is left hanging. It's an easy way to make the end of a verse feel unresolved, like a question nobody answered.

Make it yours

  • Slow it down. The same progression at 70 BPM feels much sadder than at 120.
  • Hold the minor chords longer and pass quickly through the major ones.
  • Add color: try Am7 instead of Am, or Fmaj7 instead of F. Sevenths make sadness softer and more nostalgic.
  • Play less. Single notes or slow arpeggios often hurt more than full strumming.
