Borrowed chords: the Beatles’ favorite trick

Some songs have one chord that makes you stop and feel something. Very often it’s a chord “borrowed” from outside the key, and the Beatles were masters at placing it exactly where it hurts (in a good way).

6 min read · The Chordseed Journal · Leer en español

What is a borrowed chord?

Every major key has a twin: the minor key that starts on the same note. C major and C minor share the same home, but C minor has darker notes (E♭, A♭ and B♭). A borrowed chord is a chord you take from that dark twin and drop into your happy major song.

The result is a moment of shadow, nostalgia or power without leaving home. Musicians also call it modal mixture, but you don’t need the name to use it.

The four borrowed chords worth knowing

ChordIn CIn GHow it feels
minor ivFmCmBittersweet, nostalgic. A lump in the throat.
♭VIIB♭FBig, open, a bit rebellious. Pure rock.
♭VIA♭E♭Epic and cinematic, like the sun breaking through.
♭IIIE♭B♭Bold and bright, a sudden change of light.

How the Beatles used them

The minor iv in “In My Life”

In the verses (key of A), the harmony goes to D, the normal IV chord, and then turns it into D minor before landing back on A. That one note changing, from F♯ to F, is what gives the song its gentle sadness about the past.

ADDmAIV → iv → I

The ♭VII in the coda of “Hey Jude”

The long sing-along ending (key of F) repeats a loop with E♭, a chord that isn’t in F major. It makes the ending feel huge and communal, like a stadium singing together.

FE♭B♭FI → ♭VII → IV → I

The ♭III and ♭VII in “Here Comes the Sun”

In the bridge (key of A), George Harrison cycles through C and G, two chords borrowed from A minor, before arriving at D and A. It feels like clouds moving across the sky before the light returns.

CGDA♭III → ♭VII → IV → I

How to use them in your own songs

  • Minor iv right before home. Play IV, then the same chord in minor, then I. It works in almost any ballad: C – F – Fm – C.
  • ♭VII – IV – I for a big ending. Perfect for the last chorus or a sing-along outro: G – F – C – G in the key of G.
  • ♭VI – ♭VII – I for an epic arrival. The “hero wins” move: A♭ – B♭ – C.
  • One at a time. A borrowed chord is a spice. Use it once in a section, then go back to the normal family so the surprise keeps its power.

The best test is your ear: play your progression with and without the borrowed chord and keep the version that gives you goosebumps.
